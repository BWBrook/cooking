# Medieval Oat Gruels

## Question

How should `Drawyn grwel` and `Gruelle a-forsydde` be interpreted and adapted
for the current pantry, especially given the choice between steel-cut and
instant oats?

## Short Answer

Use plain instant oats for the first practical reconstruction. The Harleian
recipes point to finely worked oatmeal, long enough cooking to soften it, and
straining before service. Instant oats give a closer smooth, drawn texture than
intact steel-cut oats unless the steel-cut oats are ground or blended first.

Make the recipes as thin, strained, savoury meat gruels, not as modern thick
breakfast porridge. The low-gas household path is to make meat and broth in the
slow cooker, then finish the oat gruel in the microwave or with a brief
saucepan cook.

## Historical Anchors

Austin's edition places both recipes in the `Potage Dyvers` section of
Harleian MS 279. The table of contents identifies `Gruelle a-forcyd` as recipe
VII and `Drawyn grwelle` as recipe XXIV.

`Gruelle a-forcyd` is an oatmeal and pork preparation. The sequence is:

- grind the oatmeal small,
- boil it well with pork,
- remove skin and bones,
- chop and pound the pork,
- combine pork and gruel,
- strain,
- return to the pot briefly,
- salt evenly,
- colour with saffron,
- serve runny.

`Drawyn grwel` is a beef, oatmeal, parsley, and sage gruel. The sequence is:

- boil lean beef in water,
- pick the cooked beef,
- return beef, blood, oatmeal, parsley, and sage to the pot,
- make a good gruel,
- strain it,
- boil again,
- salt,
- add more blood before straining if the colour is not brown enough.

## Naming and Interpretation

The user-supplied spelling `Gruelle a-forsydde` is a useful working title, but
Austin's text shows the form `Gruelle a-forcyd`. The Anglo-Norman `aforcer`
family supports readings such as strengthened, reinforced, increased, or
seasoned. For kitchen purposes, this gruel is best treated as "strengthened"
or "enriched" gruel: oatmeal reinforced with pork and coloured with saffron.

`Drawyn` is operationally clear in the recipe itself: the gruel is passed
through a strainer. That drawn texture matters. A bowl of chunky oatmeal with
meat is a valid modern dinner, but it is less source-like.

## Oat Choice

### Plain Instant Oats

Best for the first trial. They are already finely cut/processed, hydrate fast,
blend smooth, and strain reasonably well.

### Steel-Cut Oats

Interesting but not the first choice. They are coarse pieces of oat groat, so
they resist the smooth, strained texture unless ground first or cooked much
longer and blended. They are better for a modern oat-risotto or savoury
porridge than for this particular gruel family.

### Rolled Oats or Oat Flour

Rolled oats would also work if blended first. Oat flour could become gluey and
is less clearly the right texture unless used cautiously.

## Blood in Drawyn Grwel

The beef version is the more difficult adaptation because the source uses blood
for colour and body. Fresh blood is not a normal supermarket ingredient and
should not be assumed safe or available. The house baseline therefore omits it.

If the user wants to explore a darker version later, black pudding or blood
sausage is a safer modern route, though it introduces spices, fat, and casing
that are not identical to the manuscript procedure.

## Adaptation Logic

The practical historical baseline should prioritise:

- oatmeal as the structural base,
- meat broth as the liquid,
- finely worked meat,
- straining,
- a thin/runny final texture,
- saffron colour for the pork version,
- parsley and sage for the beef version.

The modern household adaptation should also respect:

- no long gas simmering,
- no nonstick dependence,
- one-person batch scale,
- Garmin-friendly batch and portion notes,
- clear separation between source-like and more filling modern service.

- Does `Drawyn grwel` need fresh parsley, or are dried parsley flakes
  acceptable in this context?
- Is a black-pudding darkening experiment worth trying later, or should the
  blood-colour element remain historical context only?
