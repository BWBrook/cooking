# Medieval Pease Pottage

## Question

How should a historically anchored pease pottage be adapted for the current
pantry and a large slow cooker?

## Short Answer

Use dry split peas as the structural base, onion and herbs as the historical
vegetable/aromatic core, a little saffron-colour and optional sweetness as the
`Forme of Cury` courtly note, and a modest bacon accent from later English
pease-pottage tradition. The slow cooker is a modern stand-in for long gentle
pot cooking and fits the household preference for low-attention cooking without
long gas use.

## Historical Anchors

`The Forme of Cury` includes `Perrey of Pesoun`, a pea puree in which peas are
boiled until they burst, worked smooth, then cooked with onions, oil, sugar,
salt, and saffron. The same section includes another pea dish, `Frenche`, built
from white peas, herbs, onions, oil, salt, saffron, and a finishing sweet spice
powder.

Those recipes are not rustic peasant recipes in a simple sense: `The Forme of
Cury` is associated with the courtly kitchens of Richard II. Still, they show
that peas cooked to a puree or pottage with onion, herbs, oil, saffron, and
spices belong naturally in late-medieval English cookery.

Robert May's seventeenth-century `Pottage in the English Fashion` gives the
later English bacon-and-herb line: old peas cooked in water with bacon and mint
or other sweet herbs. His `Dry or old Pease Pottage` also keeps the old-pea,
mint, butter, and pepper pattern.

## Adaptation Logic

### Peas

The current pantry has dry green split peas in quantity. They are not a perfect
medieval ingredient stand-in, but they behave well for a thick pottage and are
much closer in spirit to stored field peas than fresh garden peas.

### Bacon

The bacon is not required by the `Forme of Cury` pea puree, but it is strongly
anchored in later English pease-pottage tradition. For this repo, bacon should
be used as a delectable garnish/accent rather than the main bulk.

### Herbs

The historical texts point toward herbs broadly, with mint especially visible
in Robert May. The current pantry supports mint, thyme, sage, parsley flakes,
bay, and other dried herbs. Fresh mint or parsley would be excellent if bought
on demand, but the first trial can be pantry-led.

### Saffron, Sweetness, and Spice

The `Forme of Cury` pea recipes use saffron and, in one case, sugar. The
current pantry has imitation yellow saffron, which should be treated mainly as
a colour note. A tiny amount of sugar can be included as a historically
anchored softening note, but the dish should not become sweet.

`Powder douce` in the related pea-and-herb recipe suggests that a courtly
variant could finish with a very small pinch of sweet spice, such as cinnamon
and ginger. This should be optional for the first trial.

### Equipment

The slow cooker is modern, but it matches the desired cooking ecology:

- long elapsed cooking is welcome,
- long continuous gas stovetop cooking is not,
- the `5.5` to `6 L` slow cooker supports a useful batch size,
- split peas benefit from steady low heat.

## Practical Implications

- First trial should be thick, spoonable, and meal-sized rather than a thin
  soup.
- Use low or no added salt until the bacon contribution is known.
- Keep bacon modest.
- Record finished batch weight and portion weights for Garmin tracking.
- If the first version works, future variants can explore:
  - more mint,
  - a no-bacon Lenten-style version,
  - a more courtly saffron/sweet-spice version,
  - a more rustic bacon-and-bread version,
  - use of a future stick blender for smoother texture.
