# Medieval Herbolace / Erbolate

## Question

How should a small medieval-style herb-and-egg dish be adapted as the second,
smaller meal alongside the slow-cooker pease pottage?

## Short Answer

Make a baked herb-and-egg cake: eggs beaten with a heavy hand of fresh herbs,
backed up by a few pantry dried herbs, then baked in butter in a Pyrex dish or
cast-iron skillet. This fits the medieval `erbolate` / `herbolade` family well,
uses the household's abundant eggs, avoids nonstick cookware, and gives a
lighter green counterpoint to pease pottage.

## Naming Note

There is some naming mess here, which is normal for medieval recipes. In
Pegge's printed `Forme of Cury`, `Hebolace` is an herb-and-onion broth or
pottage rather than the egg cake. The egg-and-herb baked dish appears in the
same broader source tradition under names commonly transcribed as `Erbolate`
or `Herbolade`.

For this repo, `Herbolace` is a convenient working name because it captures the
intended medieval herb-dish family, but the recipe itself is more precisely a
modern adaptation of the `Erbolate` / `Herbolade` egg-and-herb bake.

## Historical Anchor

The `Erbolate` recipe directs the cook to take herbs such as parsley, mint,
savory, sage, fennel, and other stronger medicinal/aromatic herbs, chop and
grind them small, mix them with eggs, put butter in a dish, add the mixture,
bake it, and serve it.

The herb list is much stronger and more various than a modern omelette herb
sprinkle. The key historical idea is not "eggs with a garnish of herbs", but a
green herb preparation held together with eggs.

## Adaptation Logic

### Eggs

The user has active laying hens, so eggs are a genuine stock strength. A
six-egg version gives a small meal that can be eaten fresh and once as
leftovers without competing with the larger pease-pottage batch.

### Herbs

The historical herb list includes several herbs that are uncommon or not wise
to use casually today, such as rue and southernwood. The practical adaptation
should use a safe modern green-herb mix:

- fresh parsley,
- fresh mint,
- chives or spring onion greens,
- a little fresh or dried sage,
- a little thyme or marjoram,
- optional fennel fronds if available.

The current pantry can support the dish with dried mint, sage, thyme, parsley
flakes, chives, marjoram, and black pepper, but the dish will be much better
with at least one or two fresh herbs bought on demand.

### Equipment

The original instruction is to bake the egg-herb mixture in butter in a dish.
For this kitchen, the best fits are:

- a buttered Pyrex dish,
- a buttered cast-iron skillet,
- the electric fan-forced oven.

This avoids nonstick cookware and uses no sustained gas.

### Flavour

Keep it green, savoury, and peppery. A small amount of nutmeg or ginger can
provide a medieval-ish spice note, but it should not dominate. A sharp yogurt,
preserved lemon, or caper side is a modern serving choice rather than a strict
reconstruction.

## Practical Implications

- Use a lot of herbs relative to eggs.
- Bake gently enough to avoid rubbery eggs.
- Serve with toast, sippets, or a small salad/green side.
- Track egg count and finished portion weight for Garmin.
- First trial should stay simple; future versions can explore more aggressive
  herb combinations.
